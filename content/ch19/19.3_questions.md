## Part 1: Determinants of Sprint Speed: Stride Kinematics and Ground Reaction Forces

### Multiple-Choice Questions (MCQs)

1. What is the classic kinematic definition of sprint speed?  
A. Stride length divided by flight duration  
B. The product of stride length and stride frequency  
C. Rate of force development multiplied by body weight  
D. Vertical displacement divided by swing leg turnover time  

**Answer: B. The product of stride length and stride frequency**  
**Rationale:** The classic definition of sprint speed concerns the relationship between stride length and stride frequency; sprint speed can be increased by an increase in stride length or an increase in stride frequency.

2. According to modern sprinting literature, what single component is considered the most critical differentiator between elite and novice sprinters?

B. The amount of vertical force applied to the ground during the stance phase in the shortest period possible (RFD)  
C. The degree of forward trunk lean maintained during the flight phase  
D. The absolute circumference of the athlete's thigh musculature  

**Answer: B. The amount of vertical force applied to the ground during the stance phase in the shortest period possible (RFD)**  
**Rationale:** Literature on sprinting suggests that the amount of vertical force applied to the ground during the stance phase may be the most critical component to improving speed, and these greater forces must be applied in the shortest period possible (RFD).

### Applied Short-Answer Questions

20. State the classic mathematical equation for sprint speed, and explain why vertical force production—rather than swing leg repositioning time—is the primary differentiator between elite and novice sprinters.  
**Model Answer:** The classic equation for sprint speed is: Sprint Speed = Stride Length × Stride Frequency. While it might seem intuitive that faster runners simply move their legs through the air faster, biomechanical research reveals that the time required to reposition the swing leg through the air is virtually identical between elite sprinters and novice sprinters. Instead, the primary differentiator lies in ground force application: elite sprinters apply significantly greater magnitudes of vertical ground reaction force during the stance phase, and they apply these forces in the shortest time possible (extremely high Rate of Force Development, RFD). Because elite sprinters exert massive force rapidly, they require less ground contact time to displace their body mass, which simultaneously produces longer strides (2.70 m vs. 2.56 m) and higher stride rates (4.63 steps/s vs. 4.43 steps/s).  
**Evaluation Criteria:**  
- State the sprint speed equation: Sprint Speed = Stride Length × Stride Frequency.  
- Explain that swing leg repositioning time in the air is similar between elite and novice sprinters.  
- Identify stance phase vertical force magnitude and rate of force development (RFD) as the primary differentiator.  
- Note that greater vertical force in less ground contact time drives both longer strides and higher stride frequency.

21. Explain Mann's model regarding knee height at maximal flexion of the recovery leg, and describe how this technical feature influences ground clearance, force orientation, and running velocity.  
**Model Answer:** According to Mann, elite sprinters achieve an optimized, higher knee position at maximal flexion of the recovering swing leg during the flight phase. This elevated knee position provides two decisive mechanical benefits: first, it establishes greater vertical excursion and ground clearance for the recovery foot, preventing premature foot strike and tripping; second, it provides a greater vertical time window and downward trajectory to accelerate the foot toward the track. This enables the athlete to direct massive vertical forces downward into the running surface at initial contact, allowing elite sprinters to produce the vast majority of their vertical force within the first half of the stance phase. This front-loaded force profile enables elite male sprinters to achieve peak velocities of approximately 12.55 m/s, compared to novice runners who reach only ~11.25 m/s due to delayed or insufficient force transmission.  
**Evaluation Criteria:**  
- Define Mann's technical feature: optimized (higher) knee height at maximal flexion of the recovery leg.  
- Explain the role of ground clearance in preventing premature foot strike.  
- Describe how high knee flexion allows effective downward vertical force direction.  
- Detail the kinetic outcome: front-loaded force production during the first half of the stance phase, enabling peak velocities near 12.55 m/s vs. 11.25 m/s in novices.

### Applied Short-Answer Questions

20. Outline the mechanical subtasks and stride phases of linear sprinting, detailing the distinct periods that occur during ground contact (stance) and flight.  
**Model Answer:** Linear sprinting is organized into three distinct subtasks: the start, acceleration, and top speed (maximum velocity). Each stride cycle within these subtasks consists of two coupled phases: the stance (support) phase and the flight phase. The stance phase begins at initial foot contact and is divided into an initial eccentric braking period (where kinetic forces are absorbed and the center of mass moves over the foot) followed immediately by an explosive concentric propulsive period (where ground reaction forces project the body forward and upward). The flight phase occurs while the body is airborne and consists of two segments for the swing leg: the recovery segment (characterized by rapid hip and knee flexion to bring the leg forward) and the ground preparation segment (where the leg extends downward and backward in an active strike to prepare for the subsequent stance phase).  
**Evaluation Criteria:**  
- List the three subtasks: start, acceleration, top speed.  
- Detail the stance phase: eccentric braking period followed by concentric propulsive period.  
- Detail the flight phase: swing leg recovery segment and ground preparation segment.  
- Describe how these coupled phases function together to displace mass down the track.

21. Explain the technical error of overstriding, identifying two common causes and detailing why it is mechanically detrimental to sprint performance and injury prevention.  
**Model Answer:** Overstriding occurs when a sprinter's foot lands excessively far in front of their center of mass at initial ground contact. This error commonly stems from two sources: improper coaching drills (such as instructing runners to reach for successively longer lines or markers on the track to force longer strides) or an athlete's psychological panic attempting to "catch up" to a competitor by stretching out their strides. Overstriding is mechanically detrimental because it directs ground reaction forces backward against the athlete, generating large, unwanted braking forces that rapidly dissipate forward velocity, prolong ground contact time, and disrupt the natural stretch-shortening cycle. From an injury prevention standpoint, this abrupt braking contact places extreme eccentric tensile strain on the hamstring complex while extended, significantly elevating the risk of acute hamstring muscle strains.  
**Evaluation Criteria:**  
- Define overstriding (foot landing excessively far ahead of the center of mass).  
- Identify two causes: flawed coaching cues (reaching for marks on track) and athlete panic/trying to catch up.  
- Detail the mechanical detriment: generates excessive braking forces, prolongs ground contact time, dampens forward velocity.  
- Explain the injury consequence: places severe eccentric strain on hamstrings, increasing strain injury risk.
