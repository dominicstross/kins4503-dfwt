# Section 1.1: Musculoskeletal System — Assessment Questions

## Part 1: Skeletal Architecture, Joint Articulations, and Bone Modeling Dynamics

### Multiple-Choice Questions (MCQs)

1. How do skeletal muscles physically exert force to produce human movement?  
A. By pushing directly against external objects  
B. By pulling against bones that rotate about joint axes to transmit force to the environment  
C. By expanding hydraulically within the joint capsule  
D. By generating electrical repulsions against articulating cartilage  

**Answer: B. By pulling against bones that rotate about joint axes to transmit force to the environment**  
**Rationale:** Muscles can only pull, not push. Through the system of bony levers, muscle pulling forces can be manifested as either pulling or pushing forces against external objects by pulling on bones that rotate about joints.

2. Approximately how many bones comprise the human skeleton?  
A. 156 bones  
B. 206 bones  
C. 286 bones  
D. 356 bones  

**Answer: B. 206 bones**  
**Rationale:** The human body contains approximately 206 bones, providing leverage, structural support, and organ protection.

3. Which of the following skeletal structures is categorized as part of the axial skeleton?  
A. Scapula and clavicle  
B. Vertebral column (C1 through coccyx)  
C. Pelvic girdle (coxal bones)  
D. Femur and patella  

**Answer: B. Vertebral column (C1 through coccyx)**  
**Rationale:** The axial skeleton consists of the skull (cranium), vertebral column (vertebra C1 through the coccyx), ribs, and sternum. The scapula, clavicle, pelvic girdle, and limb bones belong to the appendicular skeleton.

4. How are fibrous joints structurally characterized in terms of movement capability?  
A. They allow virtually no movement  
B. They allow limited movement through flexible cartilage  
C. They allow wide, multi-directional rotation with synovial fluid  
D. They allow frictionless gliding across three perpendicular axes  

**Answer: A. They allow virtually no movement**  
**Rationale:** Fibrous joints (such as the sutures of the skull) allow virtually no movement. Cartilaginous joints allow limited movement, while synovial joints allow considerable movement.

5. What are the distinguishing anatomical features of synovial joints, where most sport movements occur?  
A. Solid bone fusion with no articulating cartilage  
B. Low friction, large range of motion, articulating hyaline cartilage, and a capsule filled with synovial fluid  
C. Fibrous suture lines anchored directly to the periosteum  
D. Intervertebral fibrocartilaginous disks surrounded by endomysium  

**Answer: B. Low friction, large range of motion, articulating hyaline cartilage, and a capsule filled with synovial fluid**  
**Rationale:** Sport and exercise movements occur mainly about synovial joints, whose most important features are low friction and large range of motion, covered with smooth hyaline cartilage and enclosed in a synovial fluid-filled capsule.

6. Which joint classification correctly describes the elbow joint and its primary rotational axis?  
A. Biaxial joint rotating about two perpendicular axes  
B. Multiaxial joint rotating about three spatial axes  
C. Uniaxial joint operating as a hinge rotating about only one axis  
D. Non-axial joint permitting only gliding movement  

**Answer: C. Uniaxial joint operating as a hinge rotating about only one axis**  
**Rationale:** Uniaxial joints, such as the elbow, operate as hinges, essentially rotating about only one axis. (Note: the knee is often referred to as a hinge joint, but its axis of rotation changes throughout its range of motion).

7. What is the correct anatomical breakdown and count of the human vertebral column from superior to inferior?  
A. 5 cervical, 10 thoracic, 7 lumbar, 4 sacral, 3 coccygeal  
B. 7 cervical, 12 thoracic, 5 lumbar, 5 sacral (fused), 3 to 5 coccygeal  
C. 8 cervical, 12 thoracic, 6 lumbar, 4 sacral, 2 coccygeal  
D. 7 cervical, 14 thoracic, 4 lumbar, 5 sacral, 5 coccygeal  

**Answer: B. 7 cervical, 12 thoracic, 5 lumbar, 5 sacral (fused), 3 to 5 coccygeal**  
**Rationale:** The vertebral column consists of 7 cervical vertebrae (neck), 12 thoracic vertebrae (middle to upper back), 5 lumbar vertebrae (lower back), 5 sacral vertebrae (fused, posterior pelvis), and 3 to 5 coccygeal vertebrae.

8. Why is it essential to systematically vary training stimuli (frequency, intensity, and exercise type) to optimize adult bone density adaptations?  
A. Bone mineral content decreases if loaded more than once per month  
B. The adaptation period of bone is longer than that of skeletal muscle  
C. Bone cells can only remodel when exercises are performed in water  
D. Heavy resistance training permanently reduces osteoblast activity  

**Answer: B. The adaptation period of bone is longer than that of skeletal muscle**  
**Rationale:** Because the adaptation period of bone is longer than that of skeletal muscle, it is important to vary the stimulus in terms of frequency, intensity, and type to foster ongoing skeletal modeling.

### Applied Short-Answer Questions

20. Explain the mechanical mechanism by which skeletal muscle tension produces both pulling and pushing forces against external objects in the environment.  
**Model Answer:** Skeletal muscles are physiologically limited to contractile tension; they can only pull, never push. Muscles exert force by pulling on their tendinous attachments, which anchor into the periosteum of bones. These bones serve as rigid levers that rotate about junctions called joints. When muscles contract and pull on bones, the resulting rotation of bony levers around joint axes transmits force through the skeletal system to the external environment. Depending on the arrangement of the bony levers and the direction of limb displacement, an internal pulling force developed by muscle fibers can manifest externally as either a pulling movement (such as a barbell row) or a pushing movement (such as a bench press or squat).  
**Evaluation Criteria:**  
- State clearly that muscles can only pull, not push.  
- Explain that muscle contraction pulls on bones via tendons.  
- Describe that bones act as levers rotating around joint axes.  
- Explain that the configuration of bony lever systems allows internal pulling forces to manifest as external pushing or pulling forces.

21. Identify the primary loading factors that stimulate bone modeling in adults, and explain why training frequency and stimulus variation are critical for long-term skeletal adaptations.  
**Model Answer:** Adult bone density and bone mineral content are positively stimulated when the skeleton is subjected to mechanical loading from heavy resistance training or explosive, high-power movements involving impact and hard landings (such as gymnastics). Key factors influencing bone modeling include the magnitude of the load, whether the load is applied along the longitudinal axis of the axial skeleton (e.g., squats or overhead presses), and the frequency of loading sessions. Stimulus variation across frequency, intensity, and exercise modality is critical because the biological adaptation cycle of bone is substantially longer than that of skeletal muscle. Varying the training stimulus prevents biological accommodation, ensures continuous mechanical strain on bone tissue, and allows sufficient remodeling time to prevent overuse stress fractures while maximizing bone mineral accretion.  
**Evaluation Criteria:**  
- Identify heavy resistance training loads and explosive/high-impact movements as primary osteogenic stimuli.  
- Note the importance of loading the axial skeleton.  
- Explain that the adaptation period of bone is longer than that of skeletal muscle.  
- Explain that varying frequency, intensity, and exercise type prevents accommodation and supports continuous structural remodeling.


## Part 2: Musculoskeletal Macrostructure, Connective Tissue, and Sarcomere Anatomy

### Multiple-Choice Questions (MCQs)

1. What connective tissue layer envelops the body's more than 430 skeletal muscles and is contiguous with the muscle tendon?  
A. Endomysium  
B. Perimysium  
C. Epimysium  
D. Sarcolemma  

**Answer: C. Epimysium**  
**Rationale:** Fibrous connective tissue, or epimysium, covers the body's more than 430 skeletal muscles and is contiguous with the tendons at the ends of the muscle.

2. In skeletal muscle macrostructure, what term defines a bundle of up to 150 muscle fibers, and what connective tissue encases it?  
A. Sarcomere encased by epimysium  
B. Fasciculus encased by perimysium  
C. Myofibril encased by endomysium

**Answer: B. Fasciculus encased by perimysium**  
**Rationale:** Under the epimysium, muscle fibers are grouped in bundles (fasciculi) that may consist of up to 150 fibers, with the bundles surrounded by connective tissue called perimysium.

3. How are muscle attachments characterized anatomically for limb and trunk musculature?

C. Limb muscles: anterior and posterior; Trunk muscles: superficial and deep  
D. Limb muscles: axial and appendicular; Trunk muscles: ventral and dorsal  

**Answer: B. Limb muscles: proximal and distal; Trunk muscles: superior and inferior**  
**Rationale:** Limb muscles have two attachments to bone: proximal (closer to the trunk) and distal (farther from the trunk). Trunk muscle attachments are termed superior (closer to the head) and inferior (closer to the feet).

4. What is a motor unit, and how many neuromuscular junctions exist on an individual skeletal muscle cell?

**Answer: A. A motor unit consists of a motor neuron and all the muscle fibers it innervates; each muscle cell has only one neuromuscular junction**  
**Rationale:** A motor neuron and the muscle fibers it innervates are called a motor unit. Each muscle cell has only one neuromuscular junction (motor end plate), although a single motor neuron innervates many muscle fibers.

5. What tubular cellular system terminates in vesicles near the Z-line and stores calcium ions within the muscle fiber?  
A. Transverse tubules (T-tubules)

**Answer: B. Sarcoplasmic reticulum**  
**Rationale:** Parallel to and surrounding each myofibril is an intricate system of tubules called the sarcoplasmic reticulum, which terminates as vesicles in the vicinity of the Z-lines where calcium ions are stored.

### Applied Short-Answer Questions

20. Outline the structural hierarchy of connective tissue coverings in skeletal muscle from outermost sheath to individual fiber membrane, explaining how this structural continuity facilitates force transmission to bone.  
**Model Answer:** Skeletal muscle is organized in a precise connective tissue hierarchy:  
- **1. Epimysium:** The outermost sheath of fibrous connective tissue encasing the entire muscle organ.  
- **2. Perimysium:** The intermediate connective tissue layer that groups muscle fibers into discrete bundles called fasciculi (each containing up to 150 fibers).  
- **3. Endomysium:** The delicate inner connective tissue layer that surrounds each individual muscle fiber, which is contiguous with the fiber's cell membrane (sarcolemma).  
**Force Transmission:** All three layers (epimysium, perimysium, and endomysium) are structurally contiguous with each other and converge at the muscle ends into dense tendons. These tendons anchor directly into the bone periosteum. When microscopic contractile proteins within individual muscle fibers generate tension, that force is transmitted through the endomysium, perimysium, and epimysium into the tendon, which pulls on the periosteum to displace the bony lever and produce movement.  
**Evaluation Criteria:**  
- Name and order the three connective tissue layers: epimysium (outer), perimysium (fasciculi), and endomysium (fiber).  
- Mention the sarcolemma as the fiber cell membrane.  
- Explain that all three layers are contiguous with each other and with the tendon.  
- Explain that tendons attach to bone periosteum, transmitting cellular contractile force to the skeletal lever system.

21. Describe the structural relationship between the sarcoplasmic reticulum and transverse tubules (T-tubules), explaining how their coordinated function ensures rapid, simultaneous contraction across the entire muscle fiber.  
**Model Answer:** The sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR) is an intricate system of longitudinal tubules that runs parallel to and surrounds each individual myofibril within the muscle fiber. The SR terminates in specialized storage vesicles situated in the vicinity of the sarcomere Z-lines, which store concentrated calcium ions (Ca²⁺). Transverse tubules (T-tubules) run perpendicular to the sarcoplasmic reticulum, terminating between two SR vesicles near the Z-lines. Crucially, T-tubules are directly continuous with the outer sarcolemma (cell membrane) at the surface of the muscle cell. When a motor nerve discharges an action potential across the neuromuscular junction, the electrical depolarization sweeps along the sarcolemma and travels rapidly down the T-tubules into the interior depths of the muscle fiber. Because T-tubules penetrate between outlying myofibrils, the action potential arrives nearly simultaneously across all depths of the cell. This triggers the immediate, coordinated release of stored calcium from the adjacent SR vesicles into the sarcoplasm surrounding all myofibrils, enabling uniform crossbridge attachment and synchronized muscular tension development.  
**Evaluation Criteria:**  
- Describe the sarcoplasmic reticulum as longitudinal tubules surrounding myofibrils, terminating in calcium-storing vesicles near Z-lines.  
- Describe T-tubules as running perpendicular to SR and being contiguous with the sarcolemma.  
- Explain that action potentials propagate from the sarcolemma down T-tubules deep into the fiber nearly simultaneously.  
- Explain that this triggers coordinated calcium release from SR vesicles across all myofibrils to produce synchronized contraction.


## Part 3: The Sliding-Filament Theory and Stages of Muscular Contraction

### Multiple-Choice Questions (MCQs)

1. In its simplest form, what is the core mechanism of the sliding-filament theory of muscular contraction?  
A. Myosin and actin filaments dissolve and reform at shorter lengths  
B. Actin filaments slide inward over myosin filaments, pulling Z-lines toward the sarcomere center and shortening the fiber  
C. Myosin filaments fold accordion-style while actin filaments remain completely rigid  
D. Z-lines push outward toward the sarcolemma, expanding fiber diameter  

**Answer: B. Actin filaments slide inward over myosin filaments, pulling Z-lines toward the sarcomere center and shortening the fiber**  
**Rationale:** The sliding-filament theory states that actin filaments at each end of the sarcomere slide inward on myosin filaments, pulling the Z-lines toward the center of the sarcomere and shortening the muscle fiber.

2. What fundamental factor dictates the instantaneous force production of a muscle at any given moment during contraction?  
A. The total quantity of blood flowing through the epimysium  
B. The number of myosin crossbridges bound to actin filaments cross-sectionally at that instant in time  
C. The thickness of the bone periosteum  
D. The length of the limb tendon  

**Answer: B. The number of myosin crossbridges bound to actin filaments cross-sectionally at that instant in time**  
**Rationale:** The textbook states as a Key Point that the number of crossbridges formed between actin and myosin at any instant in time dictates the force production of a muscle.

3. During the excitation–contraction coupling phase, what regulatory protein does calcium directly bind to, and what subsequent shift occurs?  
A. Calcium binds to tropomyosin, causing troponin to detach from the Z-line  
B. Calcium binds to troponin, causing a conformational shift in tropomyosin that exposes active actin binding sites  
C. Calcium binds to myosin ATPase, converting ATP directly into collagen  
D. Calcium binds to the sarcolemma, preventing crossbridge attachment  

**Answer: B. Calcium binds to troponin, causing a conformational shift in tropomyosin that exposes active actin binding sites**  
**Rationale:** Calcium has a high affinity for troponin. When calcium binds to troponin situated along the actin filament, it causes a shift in tropomyosin (which runs in the groove of the actin double helix), exposing the binding sites for myosin crossbridges.

4. What biochemical reaction provides the immediate energy for the myosin crossbridge pulling action (power stroke)?  
A. The synthesis of glycogen from blood glucose  
B. The hydrolysis of ATP to ADP and inorganic phosphate, catalyzed by myosin ATPase  
C. The oxidation of free fatty acids in the sarcoplasm  
D. The conversion of lactic acid to pyruvate  

**Answer: B. The hydrolysis of ATP to ADP and inorganic phosphate, catalyzed by myosin ATPase**  
**Rationale:** Energy for the pulling action, or power stroke, comes from hydrolysis (breakdown) of adenosine triphosphate (ATP) to adenosine diphosphate (ADP) and phosphate, catalyzed by the enzyme myosin adenosine triphosphatase (myosin ATPase).

5. What must occur at the myosin head to allow it to detach from the active actin site after completing the power stroke?  
A. Calcium must be completely removed from the muscle cell  
B. A new molecule of ATP must replace ADP on the globular myosin head  
C. Tropomyosin must permanently denature  
D. The motor neuron must fire an inhibitory impulse  

**Answer: B. A new molecule of ATP must replace ADP on the globular myosin head**  
**Rationale:** Another molecule of ATP must replace the ADP on the myosin crossbridge globular head in order for the head to detach from the active actin site and return to its original position.

6. Under what cellular conditions can the recharge phase of muscular contraction continue to cycle repeatedly?  
A. As long as calcium is absent and ADP is fully depleted  
B. As long as calcium is available in the myofibril, ATP is available for crossbridge detachment, and active myosin ATPase is present  
C. Only when the muscle is stretched to twice its resting length  
D. Only when all glycogen stores are exhausted  

**Answer: B. As long as calcium is available in the myofibril, ATP is available for crossbridge detachment, and active myosin ATPase is present**  
**Rationale:** The sequence of crossbridge cycling repeats over and over as long as calcium is available in the myofibril, ATP is available to assist in uncoupling myosin from actin, and sufficient active myosin ATPase is available.

7. What active physiological process brings about the relaxation phase when motor nerve stimulation ceases?  
A. Calcium is actively pumped back into the sarcoplasmic reticulum, allowing actin and myosin filaments to return to their unbound state  
B. Enzymes dissolve all remaining troponin molecules  
C. ATP stores are completely metabolized into carbon dioxide  
D. Myosin filaments permanently detach from the M-bridge  

**Answer: A. Calcium is actively pumped back into the sarcoplasmic reticulum, allowing actin and myosin filaments to return to their unbound state**  
**Rationale:** Relaxation occurs when motor nerve stimulation stops; calcium is pumped back into the sarcoplasmic reticulum, which prevents crossbridge binding and returns actin and myosin to their unbound state.

8. In a significantly stretched muscle fiber, why is instantaneous force generation potential markedly reduced?  
A. Overlapping actin filaments physically collide and deform the Z-lines  
B. I-bands and the H-zone are elongated, resulting in low crossbridge–actin alignment and fewer binding opportunities  
C. Troponin permanently detaches from the actin double helix  
D. The sarcoplasmic reticulum cannot store calcium at longer lengths  

**Answer: B. I-bands and the H-zone are elongated, resulting in low crossbridge–actin alignment and fewer binding opportunities**  
**Rationale:** In a stretched muscle, the I-bands and H-zone are elongated, causing reduced overlap and alignment between myosin crossbridges and actin binding sites, thereby lowering force potential.

### Applied Short-Answer Questions

20. Describe the five sequential phases of muscular contraction according to the sliding-filament theory, outlining the key molecular and bioenergetic events in each phase.  
**Model Answer:** The sliding-filament theory outlines five distinct, sequential phases of muscular contraction and relaxation:  
- **Phase 1 (Resting):** Under resting conditions, little calcium is present in the myofibril as it is sequestered in the sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR). Actin binding sites are covered by tropomyosin, and actin and myosin interact only in a weak, non-tension-producing bond.  
- **Phase 2 (Excitation–Contraction Coupling):** When an electrical action potential travels down the T-tubules, it triggers the release of calcium ions from the SR into the myofibril. Calcium binds with troponin, causing a conformational shift in tropomyosin that uncovers the active binding sites along actin. Myosin crossbridge heads rapidly attach to actin, establishing strong bonds.  
- **Phase 3 (Contraction / Power Stroke):** Myosin ATPase catalyzes the breakdown (hydrolysis) of ATP into ADP and inorganic phosphate, releasing energy that fuels the crossbridge flexion or power stroke, pulling the actin filament toward the center of the sarcomere. A fresh ATP molecule must bind to the myosin head to allow it to detach from actin and recock.  
- **Phase 4 (Recharge):** This cycle of crossbridge binding, power stroke, ATP-mediated detachment, and recocking repeats continuously across the myofibril as long as calcium remains present in the sarcoplasm, ATP is available, and active myosin ATPase is present.  
- **Phase 5 (Relaxation):** When motor nerve stimulation stops, calcium is actively pumped back into the sarcoplasmic reticulum. Deprived of calcium, troponin and tropomyosin return to their inhibitory positions, covering actin binding sites and returning actin and myosin to their unbound resting state.  
**Evaluation Criteria:**  
- Accurately name all five phases: Resting, Excitation–Contraction Coupling, Contraction, Recharge, and Relaxation.  
- Explain calcium storage in SR and weak resting bond in the Resting Phase.  
- Explain calcium binding to troponin, tropomyosin shifting, and crossbridge attachment in Excitation–Contraction Coupling.  
- Detail ATP hydrolysis by myosin ATPase powering the power stroke, and fresh ATP binding for detachment in Contraction.  
- State conditions for repeated crossbridge cycling in the Recharge Phase.  
- Explain active calcium reuptake into SR and return to unbound state in the Relaxation Phase.

21. Explain how sarcomere starting length dictates force production potential according to the sliding-filament theory, contrasting stretched, resting-length, and contracted muscle states.  
**Model Answer:** The magnitude of instantaneous muscular force depends directly on the number of myosin crossbridges bound to actin filaments cross-sectionally at that moment. Consequently, the initial starting length of the sarcomere determines crossbridge alignment and force potential:  
- **Stretched Muscle:** When a muscle is stretched beyond its resting length, the sarcomere elongates, pulling the Z-lines farther apart and widening both the I-bands and the H-zone. In this elongated state, there is minimal overlap between thick myosin and thin actin filaments. Because few actin binding sites align with myosin crossbridge heads, few crossbridges can bind, resulting in low force generation potential.  
- **Optimal (Resting) Length:** At normal resting or partially contracted sarcomere length (~2.5 µm), there is optimal overlap and spatial alignment between myosin crossbridge heads and actin binding sites. This geometry maximizes the cross-sectional number of crossbridges that can bind simultaneously, allowing the muscle to produce its highest potential force.  
- **Contracted Muscle:** When the muscle contracts excessively past resting length, the actin filaments slide deeply toward the sarcomere center and overlap with one another across the M-bridge. This overlapping actin interferes with optimal crossbridge binding geometry. Furthermore, the ends of the thick myosin filaments collide with the Z-lines, creating mechanical resistance. As a result, crossbridge binding potential drops and instantaneous force production decreases significantly.  
**Evaluation Criteria:**  
- State the foundational rule: force production is dictated by the cross-sectional number of bound crossbridges.  
- Explain Stretched Muscle: elongated I-bands and H-zone, minimal overlap, few crossbridges bound, low force potential.  
- Explain Optimal (Resting) Length: maximal crossbridge-actin alignment, optimal overlap, peak force potential.  
- Explain Contracted Muscle: actin filaments overlap each other, collision with Z-lines, reduced crossbridge alignment, low force potential.
