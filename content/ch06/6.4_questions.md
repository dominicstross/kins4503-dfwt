### Applied Short Answer Questions (SA)

[sa]

**Clinical/Athletic Prompt:** A collegiate distance runner travels from sea level to a training camp located at 7,500 feet (2,300 m). During their first workout on Day 2, the athlete notes an abnormally high heart rate during a routine submaximal run and experiences rapid breathing. Explain the physiological mechanisms responsible for these acute responses and outline the expected timeline for acclimatization.

**Model Answer:** Upon exposure to 7,500 feet, the reduced ambient barometric pressure decreases the partial pressure of oxygen (PO2), reducing arterial oxygen saturation. To maintain peripheral tissue oxygenation, the respiratory center triggers hyperventilation (increased breathing frequency). Simultaneously, submaximal cardiac output surges by 30% to 50%, driven entirely by an elevated heart rate to compensate for reduced oxygen content per unit of blood. Over the first 10 to 14 days, heart rate and cardiac output begin returning toward baseline as red blood cell production increases. Full chronic acclimatization will require 3 to 6 weeks, resulting in a 30% to 50% increase in red blood cell volume and enhanced muscle capillarization.

**Evaluation Criteria:**
- Identifies reduced ambient PO2 and lower arterial oxygen saturation as the primary trigger.
- Explains that acute cardiac output surge is driven by increased heart rate (not stroke volume).
- Outlines the acclimatization timeline (10–14 days for initial stabilization, 3–6 weeks for full chronic acclimatization).

---

[sa]

**Clinical/Athletic Prompt:** An endurance coach asks why an athlete's hematological gains from a 4-week altitude camp disappear several weeks after returning to sea level. Explain the mechanisms of de-acclimatization and acid-base balance shifts upon return to sea level.

**Model Answer:** Adaptations to altitude—such as elevated erythropoietin (EPO) levels, a 30% to 50% increase in red blood cell mass, and elevated hemoglobin concentration—are physiological responses to environmental hypoxia. When the athlete returns to sea level, atmospheric PO2 and arterial oxygen saturation return to normal, removing the hypoxic stimulus for elevated EPO secretion. Consequently, red blood cell production rates normalize, and excess red blood cells undergo natural turnover. Within approximately one month at sea level, total red blood cell volume and hemoglobin concentrations revert to pre-exposure baselines. Additionally, respiratory exhalation rates and renal bicarbonate excretion readjust to sea-level blood pH dynamics.

**Evaluation Criteria:**
- Explains that altitude adaptations depend on continuous hypoxic stimuli.
- States that hematological values revert to sea-level baselines within approximately 1 month.
- Identifies normalization of EPO secretion and red blood cell turnover as key mechanisms.

**Rationale:** Under normal atmospheric conditions at sea level, arterial hemoglobin is already 95% to 98% saturated with oxygen. Inhaling hyperoxic gas can only dissolve a tiny additional fraction of oxygen in plasma, providing virtually no meaningful ergogenic benefit.

**Rationale:** Nicotine causes immediate constriction of the smooth muscle surrounding bronchioles and paralyzes respiratory cilia, impeding the removal of mucus and particulate debris from the respiratory tract.

### Applied Short Answer Questions (SA)

[sa]

**Clinical/Athletic Prompt:** A collegiate athlete who smokes cigarettes experiences premature fatigue and elevated heart rates during submaximal conditioning drills compared to nonsmoking teammates. Explain the airway and cardiovascular mechanisms responsible for these impairments.

**Model Answer:** Cigarette smoking impairs performance through dual respiratory and cardiovascular mechanisms. First, nicotine causes acute bronchiole constriction and paralyzes respiratory cilia, leading to fluid and debris accumulation in the airways. This increases airway resistance and the mechanical work of breathing. Second, carbon monoxide in smoke binds to hemoglobin with >200 times the affinity of oxygen, forming carboxyhemoglobin. This drastically reduces arterial oxygen-carrying capacity. To compensate for reduced blood oxygen content, the autonomic nervous system increases catecholamine release, raising resting and submaximal heart rates and blood pressure to maintain tissue oxygen delivery, leading to premature fatigue.

**Evaluation Criteria:**
- Explains bronchiole constriction and ciliary paralysis causing increased airway resistance.
- Explains carbon monoxide formation of carboxyhemoglobin reducing blood oxygen content.
- Identifies catecholamine surge and elevated submaximal heart rate as compensating mechanisms for reduced O2 transport.

---

[sa]

**Clinical/Athletic Prompt:** An sports medicine physician is evaluating an endurance cyclist suspected of abusing recombinant human erythropoietin (EPO). What physiological performance changes would indicate EPO use, and what clinical red flags and health risks must be monitored?

**Model Answer:** Performance indicators of EPO abuse include an artificial increase in maximal oxygen uptake (VO2max) by up to 11%, reduced submaximal heart rate at a given workload, lower submaximal blood lactate levels, and enhanced acid-base buffering during high-intensity exercise. Clinical red flags include an abnormally high hematocrit (>50%). The primary health risk is a severe increase in blood viscosity. Hyperviscous blood increases cardiac workload and microvascular resistance, dramatically elevating the clinical risk of fatal or debilitating embolic events, including deep vein thrombosis (DVT), pulmonary embolism, myocardial infarction, and ischemic stroke, alongside systemic hypertension.

**Evaluation Criteria:**
- Identifies performance indicators: VO2max surge (up to 11%), reduced submaximal HR and blood lactate.
- Identifies elevated hematocrit and blood viscosity as primary clinical red flags.
- Lists major medical risks: DVT, pulmonary embolism, myocardial infarction, stroke, and hypertension.

**Clinical/Athletic Prompt:** A 55-year-old master endurance runner asks why their 10K race times have slowed over the past decade despite maintaining consistent weekly running mileage. Explain the physiological factors responsible for age-related declines in endurance performance and how training can mitigate these changes.

**Model Answer:** The decline in 10K race performance despite consistent training volume is driven by age-related physiological changes. First, sarcopenia (the progressive loss of muscle mass and Type I/II fiber cross-sectional area) reduces maximal force-generating capacity and muscle oxidative mass. Second, maximal heart rate declines predictably with age (~1 beat/min per year), reducing maximal cardiac output (Qmax = SV x HRmax). Third, relative body fat tends to increase, reducing power-to-weight ratio. While training cannot completely halt biological aging, maintaining high-intensity interval training and resistance exercise preserves muscle mass, mitigates sarcopenia, maintains higher stroke volume, and optimizes peripheral oxidative enzyme activity, significantly slowing the rate of decline compared to sedentary peers.

**Evaluation Criteria:**
- Identifies sarcopenia (loss of muscle mass) as a primary muscular mechanism.
- Explains age-related reduction in maximal heart rate lowering maximal cardiac output.
- Discusses how high-intensity training and strength training mitigate sarcopenia and preserve cardiovascular/muscular function.
