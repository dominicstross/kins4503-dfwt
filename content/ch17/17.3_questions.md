# Section 17.3: Step 3: Training Frequency — Assessment Questions

## Part 1: Training Status, Recovery Guidelines, and Split Routines

### Multiple-Choice Questions (MCQs)

1. What is the standard definition of resistance training frequency in exercise prescription?
A. The number of sets completed per muscle group in a single training session
B. The total mechanical work executed during a periodized microcycle
C. The number of training sessions completed in a given time period, typically one week
D. The total duration of time dedicated to resistance training during a macrocycle

**Answer: C. The number of training sessions completed in a given time period, typically one week**
**Rationale:** Training frequency is defined specifically as the number of training sessions completed within a designated time period, with one week serving as the standard time frame for resistance training programs.

2. According to general resistance training recovery guidelines, how many rest days should be scheduled between sessions that stress the same muscle groups?
A. Zero rest days, allowing daily continuous stimulation
B. At least one rest day, but no more than three rest days
C. Exactly four to five rest days between all workouts
D. At least seven days of complete neuromuscular recovery

**Answer: B. At least one rest day, but no more than three rest days**
**Rationale:** The foundational guideline dictates scheduling at least one rest or recovery day—but no more than three rest days—between training sessions that target the same muscle groups to ensure adequate repair while preventing detraining.

3. Which weekly training frequency is recommended for an untrained (beginner) athlete starting a total-body resistance training program?
A. 1 session per week
B. 2 to 3 sessions per week
C. 4 to 5 sessions per week
D. 6 to 7 sessions per week

**Answer: B. 2 to 3 sessions per week**
**Rationale:** For beginner (untrained) athletes, 2 to 3 total-body sessions per week are recommended to allow sufficient intervening recovery days between workouts as the athlete adapts to resistance training.

4. If a beginner athlete performs a total-body resistance training program 2 days per week, how should the workouts be scheduled across the week?
A. On consecutive days such as Monday and Tuesday
B. On consecutive days such as Thursday and Friday
C. Spaced evenly, such as Monday and Thursday or Tuesday and Friday
D. Restricted strictly to Saturday and Sunday

**Answer: C. Spaced evenly, such as Monday and Thursday or Tuesday and Friday**
**Rationale:** A 2-day total-body program for beginners must space the sessions evenly across the week (e.g., Monday/Thursday or Tuesday/Friday) to maintain appropriate recovery intervals between workouts without exceeding the 3-day rest limit.

5. What physiological consequence can occur if an athlete rests for more than three consecutive days between workouts targeting the same muscle groups (e.g., training only on Monday and Wednesday)?
A. Rapid myofibrillar hypertrophy supercompensation
B. Chronic elevation of resting metabolic rate
C. A decrease in the athlete's training status (detraining)
D. Acute suppression of serum cortisol concentrations

**Answer: C. A decrease in the athlete's training status (detraining)**
**Rationale:** Resting for more than three days (such as training on Monday and Wednesday and then resting until the following Monday) leaves a 4-day gap without training stimulus, which can cause a decrease in training status (detraining).

6. What exception exists regarding low training frequency for maintaining strength in well-trained athletes over a short duration?
A. Well-trained athletes require daily maximum loading to maintain strength
B. One training session per week can maintain strength for a short period
C. Training once every three weeks prevents any strength loss indefinitely
D. Strength cannot be maintained if frequency drops below 4 sessions per week

**Answer: B. One training session per week can maintain strength for a short period**
**Rationale:** Research indicates that for a short period in well-trained athletes, performing just one resistance training session per week is sufficient to maintain previously acquired strength levels.

7. How does a split routine enable advanced athletes to train 4 to 7 days per week without violating recovery guidelines?
A. By performing light aerobic recovery sessions instead of lifting weights
B. By training different muscle groups or body regions on different days
C. By reducing exercise volume to one set per muscle group per week
D. By eliminating all eccentric muscle actions from every workout

**Answer: B. By training different muscle groups or body regions on different days**
**Rationale:** Split routines group exercises by body region (e.g., upper vs. lower body) or muscle groups (e.g., chest/shoulders/triceps vs. legs), allowing trained athletes to train almost daily while providing 2 to 3 days of localized recovery between similar sessions.

8. In a classic 4-day upper/lower body split routine (training lower body Mon/Thu and upper body Tue/Fri), how many days of rest does each specific body region receive between sessions?
A. 0 days of rest
B. 1 day of rest
C. 2 to 3 days of rest
D. 5 days of rest

**Answer: C. 2 to 3 days of rest**
**Rationale:** In a Mon/Thu lower body and Tue/Fri upper body split, each body region receives 2 to 3 rest days between targeted workouts (e.g., lower body rests Tue/Wed/Fri/Sat/Sun), even though the athlete lifts on two consecutive days twice a week.


### Multiple-Select Questions (MSs)

9. Which of the following factors must a strength and conditioning professional evaluate when establishing resistance training frequency for an athlete? (Select all that apply)
A. The athlete's current training status and preparedness level
B. The current sport season and associated skill practice demands
C. Projected exercise loads and relative intensity
D. Types of exercises prescribed (multijoint vs. single-joint)
E. Concurrent training stress and non-sport occupational physical demands

**Answer: A, B, C, D, E**
**Rationale:** All five factors—training status, sport season, exercise load, exercise type, and concurrent athletic or occupational stress—are explicit considerations required to determine appropriate resistance training frequency.

10. Which weekly resistance training frequencies correctly match the athlete's training status according to NSCA guidelines? (Select all that apply)
A. Beginner (Untrained): 2 to 3 sessions per week
B. Intermediate (Moderately Trained): 3 to 4 sessions per week
C. Advanced (Trained): 4 to 7 sessions per week
D. Beginner (Untrained): 5 to 6 sessions per week
E. Advanced (Trained): 1 session per month

**Answer: A, B, C**
**Rationale:** Guidelines specify 2–3 sessions/week for beginners, 3–4 sessions/week for intermediate athletes, and 4–7 sessions/week for advanced athletes utilizing split routines. Beginners should not train 5–6 days/week.

11. Which split routine configurations provide appropriate recovery spacing across a microcycle? (Select all that apply)
A. Lower Body on Monday & Thursday; Upper Body on Tuesday & Friday
B. Chest/Shoulders/Triceps on Monday; Legs on Tuesday; Back/Biceps on Wednesday; repeat after rest
C. Total Body training 7 days per week with maximum load every day
D. Upper Body on Monday & Wednesday; Upper Body on Tuesday & Thursday with zero rest
E. Lower Body on Tuesday & Friday; Upper Body on Wednesday & Saturday

**Answer: A, B, E**
**Rationale:** Split routines dividing the body into upper/lower or muscle group splits (such as Mon/Thu lower and Tue/Fri upper, 3-day push/pull/legs splits, or Tue/Fri lower and Wed/Sat upper) provide localized muscle recovery. Daily maximal total-body training violates recovery principles.

12. Which statements accurately describe the physiological rules of recovery spacing between workouts? (Select all that apply)
A. At least one rest day should be scheduled between sessions targeting the same muscle groups.
B. No more than three rest days should elapse between sessions targeting the same muscle groups to avoid detraining.
C. Evenly spacing 2-day total body routines (e.g., Mon/Thu) prevents prolonged gaps.
D. Resting 5 consecutive days between workouts enhances myofibrillar protein synthesis beyond baseline.
E. Advanced athletes can train on consecutive days if different muscle groups are stressed.

**Answer: A, B, C, E**
**Rationale:** Proper spacing requires 1 to 3 rest days between same-muscle sessions. Evenly spaced 2-day routines prevent detraining gaps, and split routines permit consecutive-day lifting by rotating muscle groups. Resting 5 days exceeds the 3-day window and risks detraining.

A. True

A. True

**Answer: A. True**
**Rationale:** Research confirms that one session per week can temporarily maintain strength in well-trained athletes during brief low-frequency periods.

### Applied Short-Answer Questions

20. Explain the physiological rationale behind the rule requiring at least one rest day, but no more than three rest days, between resistance training sessions that stress the same muscle groups.
**Model Answer:** The rule balances neuromuscular recovery with adaptation maintenance. At least one rest day is required for muscle protein synthesis, tissue repair, and replenishment of metabolic substrates following microtrauma. Conversely, limiting rest to no more than three days prevents the loss of physiological adaptations (detraining). A gap exceeding three days removes the adaptive stimulus, causing performance and training status to decline.

21. Contrast a beginner's total-body training schedule with an advanced athlete's 4-day upper/lower split routine in terms of weekly frequency, session spacing, and muscle recovery.
**Model Answer:** A beginner performs a total-body routine 2 to 3 days per week (e.g., Monday/Thursday), with 48 to 72 hours of complete rest between sessions to allow systemic and localized recovery. An advanced athlete trains 4 days per week using an upper/lower split (e.g., Lower Mon/Thu, Upper Tue/Fri). Although the advanced athlete lifts on consecutive days (Mon/Tue and Thu/Fri), each specific muscle group receives 2 to 3 days of recovery between targeted workouts, permitting higher overall frequency without overtraining.


## Part 2: Sport Season Priorities and Training Frequency Adjustments

### Multiple-Choice Questions (MCQs)

1. How does the onset of the competitive in-season primarily affect resistance training frequency?
A. It increases resistance frequency to 6 sessions per week to maximize strength gains
B. It reduces resistance frequency due to increased time spent on sport skill practice
C. It eliminates all forms of physical activity outside of competitive games
D. It forces athletes to train exclusively with 1RM loads to preserve muscle mass

**Answer: B. It reduces resistance frequency due to increased time spent on sport skill practice**
**Rationale:** During the in-season, the heavy time commitment required for sport skill practice and games necessitates a decrease in weight room time, reducing resistance training frequency.

2. What is the primary goal of resistance training during the competitive in-season phase?
A. Achieving maximal muscle hypertrophy
B. Developing peak explosive power from baseline
C. Maintaining previously acquired strength and power levels
D. Inducing functional overreaching to build work capacity

**Answer: C. Maintaining previously acquired strength and power levels**
**Rationale:** In-season resistance training focuses on maintaining strength and conditioning gains made during the off-season and preseason while minimizing fatigue during competition.

3. What is the recommended resistance training frequency during the off-season microcycle for athletes?
A. 0 to 1 session per week
B. 1 to 3 sessions per week
C. 4 to 6 sessions per week
D. 7 to 8 sessions per week

**Answer: C. 4 to 6 sessions per week**
**Rationale:** The off-season is the primary period for physical development, allowing high resistance training priority with 4 to 6 sessions per week because sport practice demands are low.

4. What is the recommended resistance training frequency during the preseason phase?
A. 1 to 2 sessions per week
B. 3 to 4 sessions per week
C. 5 to 7 sessions per week
D. 0 sessions per week

**Answer: B. 3 to 4 sessions per week**
**Rationale:** Preseason resistance frequency is moderately reduced to 3 to 4 sessions per week as sport-specific skill practice and tactical conditioning increase.

5. What is the recommended resistance training frequency during the competitive in-season phase?
A. 1 to 3 sessions per week
B. 4 to 5 sessions per week
C. 6 sessions per week
D. 0 sessions per week

**Answer: A. 1 to 3 sessions per week**
**Rationale:** In-season resistance training is limited to 1 to 3 sessions per week to preserve physical energy and prevent central nervous system fatigue during competitive play.

6. What is the recommended resistance training frequency during the active rest (post-season) period?
A. 5 to 6 sessions per week of heavy strength work
B. 0 to 3 sessions per week of light, non-specific activity
C. 4 sessions per week of high-intensity Olympic lifting
D. Daily maximal strength testing

**Answer: B. 0 to 3 sessions per week of light, non-specific activity**
**Rationale:** Active rest (post-season) involves low resistance training frequency (0 to 3 light sessions per week) to foster physical and mental recovery before off-season training resumes.

7. Why might a well-trained athlete capable of lifting 5 days per week be restricted to only 2 resistance sessions per week during the in-season?
A. The athlete loses the physiological capacity to adapt during competition
B. Total daily time constraints and sport practice volume do not permit higher gym frequency
C. Muscle tissue becomes resistant to hypertrophy during competitive play
D. Lifting more than twice per week in-season triggers immediate muscle atrophy

**Answer: B. Total daily time constraints and sport practice volume do not permit higher gym frequency**
**Rationale:** Even if an athlete is physically capable of handling high gym frequency, the daily time demands of sport practice, strategy meetings, travel, and games leave insufficient time for frequent weight room sessions.

8. As an athlete transitions from off-season to preseason and then in-season, how does the relationship between sport practice volume and resistance training frequency shift?
A. Sport practice volume decreases while resistance frequency increases
B. Both sport practice volume and resistance frequency increase simultaneously
C. Sport practice volume increases while resistance frequency decreases
D. Both sport practice volume and resistance frequency remain completely unchanged

**Answer: C. Sport practice volume increases while resistance frequency decreases**
**Rationale:** Across the macrocycle, sport skill practice volume increases toward in-season competition, requiring a proportional reduction in resistance training frequency.

### Applied Short-Answer Questions

20. Describe how resistance training frequency shifts across the four sport seasons (off-season, preseason, in-season, and active rest), explaining the rationale for each phase.
**Model Answer:** 
- *Off-Season (4–6 sessions/week):* High priority; sport practice is low, allowing maximal focus on developing hypertrophy, strength, and power.
- *Preseason (3–4 sessions/week):* Moderate priority; gym frequency decreases as sport-specific conditioning and tactical practice increase.
- *In-Season (1–3 sessions/week):* Maintenance priority; gym frequency is minimized to conserve time and physical energy for competitive games.
- *Active Rest (0–3 sessions/week):* Recovery priority; low-frequency, light activity fosters physical and mental healing before the next off-season.

Daily schedules are consumed by skill practices, game strategy, film review, travel, and competitive games. High gym frequency would cause central nervous system fatigue and muscle soreness, impairing game performance. Reducing frequency to 1–3 sessions per week maintains physical qualities while preserving recovery for competition.


## Part 3: Training Load, Exercise Type, and Concurrent Training Stress

### Multiple-Choice Questions (MCQs)

1. How do maximal or near-maximal training loads (e.g., ≥85–90% 1RM) impact the required recovery time between resistance training sessions?
A. They shorten required recovery time because fewer repetitions are performed
B. They require more recovery time due to higher neuromuscular and metabolic stress
C. They eliminate the need for rest days between identical lifting sessions
D. They allow athletes to train the same heavy movement 7 days per week

**Answer: B. They require more recovery time due to higher neuromuscular and metabolic stress**
**Rationale:** Training with maximal or near-maximal loads places intense stress on the nervous system and muscle tissue, necessitating longer recovery periods before the next heavy session.

2. How can a strength coach structure weekly loading to allow an athlete to train frequently without experiencing overtraining from heavy loads?
A. By prescribing 100% 1RM on every exercise every day
B. By alternating lighter and heavier training days across the microcycle
C. By eliminating rest periods between sets
D. By replacing all free weights with light resistance bands

**Answer: B. By alternating lighter and heavier training days across the microcycle**
**Rationale:** Alternating heavy loading days with lighter recovery days allows athletes to maintain a higher weekly workout frequency while ensuring adequate tissue repair.

3. According to research on recovery rates between muscle groups, which muscle areas recover more quickly from heavy loading sessions?
A. Lower body muscles recover faster than upper body muscles
B. Upper body muscles recover more quickly from heavy loading than lower body muscles
C. Hip extensors recover faster than elbow flexors
D. Quadriceps recover faster than deltoids

**Answer: B. Upper body muscles recover more quickly from heavy loading than lower body muscles**
**Rationale:** Studies demonstrate that upper body muscles generally recover faster from heavy loading sessions than larger lower body muscle groups.

4. How do recovery rates differ between single-joint assistance exercises and multijoint structural/power exercises?
A. Multijoint power exercises recover much faster than single-joint assistance exercises
B. Single-joint exercises require up to 7 days of complete rest
C. Athletes recover faster from single-joint exercises than from multijoint exercises
D. Single-joint and multijoint exercises recover at identical physiological rates

**Answer: C. Athletes recover faster from single-joint exercises than from multijoint exercises**
**Rationale:** Research shows that single-joint assistance exercises cause less systemic and neural fatigue, allowing faster recovery compared to heavy multijoint exercises.

5. Why might elite powerlifters schedule only one very heavy deadlift or squat session per week?
A. Because powerlifters lack the discipline to train multiple days per week
B. Because heavy multijoint lower-body lifts impose massive neural and structural fatigue requiring extended recovery
C. Because squatting more than once a month leads to immediate tendon rupture
D. Because lower-body exercises do not contribute to competitive powerlifting totals

**Answer: B. Because heavy multijoint lower-body lifts impose massive neural and structural fatigue requiring extended recovery**
**Rationale:** Heavy lower-body multijoint lifts (squats and deadlifts) combine large muscle mass recruitment, axial loading, and near-maximal loads, creating systemic fatigue that takes up to a week to fully recover.

6. How does concurrent non-resistance training (e.g., sprinting, agility, plyometrics, aerobic conditioning) influence resistance training frequency?
A. It permits an increase in resistance frequency to 7 days per week
B. It has zero impact on weight room recovery or frequency
C. It increases total physical stress, requiring a potential reduction in resistance training frequency
D. It completely eliminates the need for rest days between heavy workouts

**Answer: C. It increases total physical stress, requiring a potential reduction in resistance training frequency**
**Rationale:** Sprinting, plyometrics, agility, and sport practice contribute to overall physical stress; if concurrent training volume is high, resistance frequency must be reduced to avoid overtraining.

7. Why must a strength professional evaluate an athlete's non-sport occupational physical activity when setting resistance training frequency?
A. Manual labor and standing occupations add non-sport physical stress that consumes recovery capacity
B. Active occupations eliminate the need for weight room warm-ups
C. Athletes who work physical jobs require double the normal resistance training volume
D. Sitting at a desk all day increases systemic physical stress more than manual labor

**Answer: A. Manual labor and standing occupations add non-sport physical stress that consumes recovery capacity**
**Rationale:** Occupational physical stress (e.g., manual labor, standing all day) draws upon the athlete's total recovery budget. Those with demanding jobs cannot withstand the same gym frequency as less active peers.

8. Which exercise selection scenario would require the LONGEST recovery interval before repeating heavy loading?
A. Single-joint upper-body assistance exercise (e.g., bicep curl)
B. Single-joint lower-body assistance exercise (e.g., calf raise)
C. Multijoint lower-body structural exercise loaded near maximal capacity (e.g., heavy back squat)
D. Multijoint upper-body assistance exercise using light loads (e.g., light lat pulldown)

**Answer: C. Multijoint lower-body structural exercise loaded near maximal capacity (e.g., heavy back squat)**
**Rationale:** Heavy multijoint lower-body structural exercises stress large muscle masses and the axial skeleton near maximal loads, requiring the longest recovery window of all exercise types.

### Applied Short-Answer Questions

20. Explain why a powerlifter might schedule a heavy back squat session only once per week, referencing exercise load, muscle mass involved, and joint involvement.
**Model Answer:** Heavy back squats involve near-maximal loads (≥85–90% 1RM) performed across multiple joints (hips and knees) recruiting massive lower-body muscle groups and loading the axial spine. This creates substantial central nervous system fatigue, structural microtrauma, and metabolic stress. Because lower-body multijoint lifts recover significantly slower than upper-body or single-joint exercises, scheduling heavy squats once per week provides the 5 to 7 days required for complete recovery and adaptation.

21. A collegiate soccer player works a summer manual labor construction job for 8 hours a day while attending team speed/agility practices 3 afternoons a week. How should the strength coach adjust resistance training frequency and microcycle structure?
