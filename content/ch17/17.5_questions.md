# Section 17.5: Step 5: Training Load and Repetitions — Assessment Questions

## Part 1: Load Terminology, Volume-Load, and Load-Repetition Relationships

### Multiple-Choice Questions (MCQs)

1. How is volume-load mathematically defined in resistance training program design?
A. The average load lifted per repetition divided by the session duration in minutes
B. The product of the weight lifted and the number of repetitions performed, summed across all sets
C. The product of force, vertical displacement distance, and total sets completed
D. The total number of repetitions completed in a workout session regardless of load

**Answer: B. The product of the weight lifted and the number of repetitions performed, summed across all sets**
**Rationale:** Volume-load is calculated by multiplying each weight lifted by the number of times it is lifted (weight x repetitions) and summing all such values over a training session.

2. How does volume-load differ from pure mechanical work?
A. Volume-load includes metabolic power, whereas mechanical work excludes internal energy expenditure
B. Volume-load omits the vertical displacement distance value but remains directly related to mechanical work and metabolic demand
C. Volume-load is measured in Joules, whereas mechanical work is measured in kilograms
D. Volume-load accounts for rest period duration, whereas mechanical work excludes rest intervals

**Answer: B. Volume-load omits the vertical displacement distance value but remains directly related to mechanical work and metabolic demand**
**Rationale:** While mechanical work includes force multiplied by displacement (distance), volume-load does not include the distance value; however, it remains directly proportional to mechanical work and physiological stress.

3. When calculating volume-load for exercise movements where the body itself is moved against gravity (e.g., loaded jump squats), how should the calculation be adjusted?
A. Multiply the barbell load by body mass and divide by total sets
B. Use system mass volume-load, calculated as: (Body Mass + Barbell Load) x Repetitions
C. Subtract body mass from the barbell load before multiplying by repetitions
D. Exclude the barbell load and multiply body mass by vertical jump height

**Answer: B. Use system mass volume-load, calculated as: (Body Mass + Barbell Load) x Repetitions**
**Rationale:** For exercises where body mass is accelerated alongside external resistance (e.g., loaded jump squats), system mass volume-load must be calculated by adding body mass to the external load before multiplying by total repetitions.

4. What parameter is obtained by dividing total volume-load by total repetition-volume (rep-volume) for a training session?
A. The total concentric displacement distance per exercise
B. The average weight lifted per repetition, serving as an approximation for mechanical and metabolic intensity
C. The neuromuscular fatigue index of the primary motor units
D. The exact rate of force development across periodized microcycles

**Answer: B. The average weight lifted per repetition, serving as an approximation for mechanical and metabolic intensity**
**Rationale:** Dividing volume-load by rep-volume results in the average weight lifted per repetition per session, which serves as a practical proxy for mechanical and metabolic power output (true training intensity/quality).

5. What is the fundamental physiological relationship between load lifted and repetitions performed?
A. Direct linear relationship; as load increases, repetitions increase proportionally
B. Inverse relationship; as load increases, the maximum achievable number of repetitions decreases
C. Independent relationship; repetitions performed are unaffected by load magnitude
D. Exponential relationship; repetitions double for every 10% increase in load

**Answer: B. Inverse relationship; as load increases, the maximum achievable number of repetitions decreases**
**Rationale:** Load and repetitions share an inverse relationship: the heavier the assigned resistance load, the lower the maximum number of repetitions that can be successfully executed.

6. According to Table 17.7 (%1RM–Repetition Relationship), how many repetitions are associated with a load equal to 85% of 1RM?
A. 2 repetitions
B. 6 repetitions
C. 10 repetitions
D. 12 repetitions

**Answer: B. 6 repetitions**
**Rationale:** Table 17.7 defines 85% of 1RM as corresponding to 6 repetitions maximum.

7. Which of the following statements represents a known limitation of using %1RM tables to establish training loads across all exercises?
A. Research proves the load-repetition relationship is strictly linear across all muscle groups
B. Trained athletes can often complete significantly more repetitions than listed at a given %1RM on lower body core exercises
C. Machine exercises consistently yield fewer repetitions than free weight exercises at identical %1RM loads
D. %1RM tables are most accurate for light loads below 50% 1RM and repetitions exceeding 20

**Answer: B. Trained athletes can often complete significantly more repetitions than listed at a given %1RM on lower body core exercises**
**Rationale:** Research shows that resistance-trained athletes can frequently exceed the repetition numbers listed in %1RM tables at a given percentage of 1RM, particularly on lower body core lifts (e.g., performing 2 to 3 times more reps).

8. At what load intensity and repetition range is the relationship between %1RM and maximum repetitions possible most accurate?
A. Loads less than 50% of 1RM and more than 15 repetitions
B. Loads greater than 75% of 1RM and fewer than 10 repetitions
C. Loads between 60% and 70% of 1RM for exactly 12 repetitions
D. Bodyweight-only loads performed to muscular failure

**Answer: B. Loads greater than 75% of 1RM and fewer than 10 repetitions**
**Rationale:** Empirical evidence and research demonstrate that the most accurate relationship between percentages of 1RM and maximum repetitions occurs for heavy loads greater than 75% of 1RM and fewer than 10 repetitions.

### Applied Short-Answer Questions

20. Explain the distinction between repetition-volume, volume-load, and system mass volume-load, detailing how each is calculated for loaded jump squats.
**Model Answer:** Repetition-volume is the total number of repetitions performed in a session (e.g., 4 sets of 3 reps = 12 reps). Volume-load multiplies weight lifted by repetitions (e.g., 40 kg barbell x 12 reps = 480 kg), serving as a proxy for mechanical work and metabolic demand without measuring displacement. System mass volume-load includes the athlete's body mass for movements accelerating body weight (e.g., [80 kg body mass + 40 kg load] x 12 reps = 1,440 kg), accurately capturing total mass moved against gravity.

21. Discuss three major physiological limitations of relying strictly on standardized %1RM-repetition tables (Table 17.7) to assign exercise loads across a diverse athlete population.
**Model Answer:** First, the load-repetition relationship is curvilinear rather than strictly linear, creating higher variability at lighter loads. Second, muscle mass involvement and exercise mode alter repetition capacity: resistance-trained athletes complete significantly more reps than listed on lower body core lifts and selectorized machines compared to upper body or free-weight exercises. Third, multi-set fatigue reduces repetition capacity across consecutive sets unless loads are adjusted, and accuracy drops substantially for loads below 75% 1RM or repetitions exceeding 10.


## Part 2: 1RM and Multiple-RM Testing Protocols

### Multiple-Choice Questions (MCQs)

1. For which athlete classification is direct 1-repetition maximum (1RM) testing primarily reserved?
A. Novice athletes in their first month of resistance training
B. Intermediate and advanced resistance-trained athletes with exercise technique experience
C. Injured athletes undergoing initial post-rehabilitation screening
D. Youth athletes under 12 years of age preparing for sport sampling

**Answer: B. Intermediate and advanced resistance-trained athletes with exercise technique experience**
**Rationale:** Direct 1RM testing places significant mechanical stress on muscles, joints, and connective tissues, and is reserved for intermediate and advanced athletes with sufficient lifting status and technique experience.

2. Which type of exercise is appropriate for direct 1RM testing?
A. Single-joint isolation exercises such as leg extensions
B. Multijoint core exercises such as the back squat and bench press
C. Exercises with weak stabilizing muscles such as the bent-over row
D. High-repetition assistance lifts such as lateral raises

**Answer: B. Multijoint core exercises such as the back squat and bench press**
**Rationale:** Core exercises involving large muscle groups and multiple joints are selected for 1RM testing because they safely tolerate heavy axial loads.

3. Why is the bent-over row exercise excluded from direct 1RM testing despite involving large muscle groups?
A. The upper back muscles are too weak to handle heavy loads
B. Fatigue in the lower back stabilizing musculature compromises technique and invalidates test safety
C. It cannot be loaded with free weight barbells
D. It involves only a single joint movement pattern

**Answer: B. Fatigue in the lower back stabilizing musculature compromises technique and invalidates test safety**
**Rationale:** While upper back muscles tolerate heavy loads, the lower back spinal stabilizers fatigue rapidly during repeated 1RM attempts, leading to loss of proper position, unsafe execution, and invalid test data.

4. What rest interval duration is recommended between sequential 1RM testing attempts?
A. 30 to 60 seconds
B. 1 to 2 minutes
C. 2 to 4 minutes
D. 7 to 10 minutes

**Answer: C. 2 to 4 minutes**
**Rationale:** Standard 1RM testing protocols require 2 to 4 minutes of rest between maximal and near-maximal attempts to allow phosphagen (ATP-CP) repletion.

5. Within how many maximal attempts should an athlete's true 1RM ideally be determined during a testing session?
A. 1 attempt
B. 3 to 5 attempts
C. 7 to 9 attempts
D. 10 to 12 attempts

**Answer: B. 3 to 5 attempts**
**Rationale:** Testing protocols aim to discover the athlete's 1RM within 3 to 5 attempts after warm-up sets to prevent fatigue from compromising maximal force production.

6. What rule governs multiple-RM testing for explosive power exercises (e.g., power clean, snatch)?
A. Power exercises should be tested at 10RM to 15RM to assess speed endurance
B. Power exercises must never be tested above 5 repetitions due to rapid technique deterioration
C. Power exercises require 20RM testing to calculate explosive velocity curves
D. Power exercises follow identical high-repetition testing protocols as assistance lifts

**Answer: B. Power exercises must never be tested above 5 repetitions due to rapid technique deterioration**
**Rationale:** Power exercises demand peak movement velocity and technical precision; testing above 5 repetitions causes rapid fatigue-induced technique breakdown, increasing injury risk and invalidating test data.

7. What maximum load intensity constraint applies when conducting multiple-RM testing or assigning loads for assistance exercises?
A. Assistance exercises must be tested and assigned at 1RM loads
B. Assistance exercises must be assigned loads at or above an 8RM (<= 8RM load) to minimize joint stress
C. Assistance exercises should only be assigned 3RM loads for hypertrophy
D. Assistance exercises have no loading limits and follow power lift protocols

**Answer: B. Assistance exercises must be assigned loads at or above an 8RM (<= 8RM load) to minimize joint stress**
**Rationale:** To protect isolated single joints and connective tissue from excessive tensional stress, assistance exercises should be assigned loads no heavier than an 8RM (8RM or lighter, i.e., >= 8 goal reps).

8. If an athlete achieves a verified 10RM of 300 pounds on the leg press, what is the estimated 1RM using Table 17.8 (%1RM = 75% for 10RM)?
A. 325 pounds
B. 350 pounds
C. 400 pounds
D. 450 pounds

**Answer: C. 400 pounds**
**Rationale:** A 10RM corresponds to 75% of 1RM in Table 17.8. Dividing 300 lbs by 0.75 yields an estimated 1RM of 400 lbs (300 / 0.75 = 400).

### Applied Short-Answer Questions

20. Contrast the selection criteria, safety considerations, and protocol guidelines for direct 1RM testing versus 10RM testing.
**Model Answer:** Direct 1RM testing requires intermediate/advanced training status, proven technique experience, and multijoint core lifts (squats, bench press) that handle heavy axial stress without stabilizer fatigue (excluding bent-over rows). It uses 2-4 minute rest periods and 5-10% upper / 10-20% lower load increases to find 1RM in 3-5 attempts. Conversely, 10RM testing is suitable for broader populations (including intermediate lifters), reduces peak joint stress, uses smaller load increments, and projects 1RM via tables/equations, but must never be applied to power lifts above 5 reps.

21. Explain the physiological rationale for capping assistance exercise loading at or above an 8RM (&le;8RM load intensity), even during a maximal strength phase.
**Model Answer:** Assistance exercises isolate single joints and smaller muscle groups. Applying heavy structural loads (e.g., 2RM to 5RM) creates excessive tensional stress on single-joint cartilage, tendons, and connective tissue without the load-sharing benefits of multijoint structures. Capping assistance loads at an 8RM (8RM or lighter, i.e., 8+ reps) protects connective tissue integrity while still driving localized hypertrophy and endurance support.

### Applied Short-Answer Questions

20. Explain why peak power output occurs at submaximal loads (75-90% 1RM) rather than 1RM loads, and how this dictates repetition maximum loading for power lifts.
**Model Answer:** Power is the product of force and velocity (P = F x V). While a 1RM produces peak force, movement velocity is extremely slow, resulting in low total power. Submaximal loads (75-90% 1RM) allow intermediate-to-high shortening velocities, maximizing mechanical power output. Furthermore, power lifts cannot be trained to true repetition maximums (failure) because movement speed and explosive technique deteriorate before muscular failure occurs. Assigning submaximal loads (e.g., 75-85% for 3-5 reps) preserves explosive velocity and motor unit firing without technical breakdown.

21. Describe how a strength coach implements microcycle load variation (Heavy/Light/Medium days) and the 2-for-2 rule to drive long-term strength gains without overtraining.
**Model Answer:** Microcycle variation manages fatigue by scheduling one Heavy Day (100% target RM load for goal reps), followed by a Light Day (80% of Heavy load for goal reps) and a Medium Day (90% of Heavy load for goal reps). This maintains training frequency and volume while allowing active tissue recovery. To progress load safely, the coach applies the 2-for-2 rule: when the athlete completes 2 or more reps over their target on the final set of the Heavy Day for 2 consecutive workouts, load is increased for the next microcycle (e.g., +5-10 lb for upper body, +10-15 lb for lower body).
