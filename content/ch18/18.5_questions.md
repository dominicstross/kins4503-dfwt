# Section 18.5: Safety Considerations — Assessment Questions

## Part 1: Pretraining Evaluation of the Athlete: Technique, Strength, Balance, and Physical Characteristics

### Multiple-Choice Questions (MCQs)

1. According to the text, under what primary circumstances do injuries typically occur during plyometric training?  
A. Because plyometric exercise is inherently dangerous and exceeds physiological tissue limits  
B. When proper training procedures are violated, such as an insufficient strength base, poor landing mechanics, or inappropriate volume and intensity  
C. Exclusively due to unavoidable random athletic accidents  
D. When athletes perform low-intensity skipping warm-up drills  

**Answer: B. When proper training procedures are violated, such as an insufficient strength base, poor landing mechanics, or inappropriate volume and intensity**  
**Rationale:** Injuries typically occur when proper training procedures are violated, which may result from an insufficient strength and conditioning base, inadequate warm-up, improper progression of lead-up drills, inappropriate volume or intensity, poor shoes or surface, or a lack of skill.

2. In the sagittal plane, what alignment defines proper landing technique during lower body plyometrics?  
A. Shoulders leaned far behind the hips with completely extended, locked knees  
B. Shoulders positioned over the knees and knees positioned over the toes, achieved through flexion of the ankles, knees, and hips  
C. Trunk hyperextended with weight placed exclusively on the lateral borders of the heels  
D. Hips fully extended with all impact absorbed by the lumbar spine  

**Answer: B. Shoulders positioned over the knees and knees positioned over the toes, achieved through flexion of the ankles, knees, and hips**  
**Rationale:** The shoulders should be over the knees and the knees over the toes during landing, which the jumper accomplishes through synchronized flexion of the ankles, knees, and hips (triple flexion) while maintaining the center of gravity over the base of support.

3. When observing an athlete's landing mechanics in the frontal plane, what alignment flaw represents a major risk factor for patellofemoral pain and ACL tears?  
A. Knee flexion greater than 45 degrees  
B. Dynamic valgus (inward movement or collapse of the knees)  
C. Ankle dorsiflexion aligned with the second toe  
D. Slight external rotation of the hip during takeoff  

**Answer: B. Dynamic valgus (inward movement or collapse of the knees)**  
**Rationale:** Inward movement of the knees—termed dynamic valgus—is a significant risk factor for knee injuries of all types, including patellofemoral pain and tears or ruptures of the anterior cruciate ligament (ACL).

4. What was the historical strength prerequisite recommended before an athlete performed lower body plyometrics?

**Answer: B. The athlete's 1RM squat should be at least 1.5 times his or her body weight**  
**Rationale:** Previous recommendations held that the athlete's 1RM squat should be at least 1.5 times his or her body weight before participating in lower body plyometrics.

5. What is the modern consensus regarding the relative importance of absolute strength versus movement technique for initiating plyometrics?  
A. Absolute 1RM squat strength is the sole determinant of plyometric safety  
B. Movement technique and proper alignment mechanics are a more important consideration than an arbitrary 1RM squat threshold

**Answer: B. Movement technique and proper alignment mechanics are a more important consideration than an arbitrary 1RM squat threshold**  
**Rationale:** The authors suggest that a more important consideration than a 1.5× body weight 1RM squat is technique; many plyometric activities can be safely taught to young and novice athletes to develop proper mechanics.

8. What safety restrictions apply to athletes who weigh more than 220 pounds (100 kg) when performing plyometrics?

### Applied Short-Answer Questions

20. Describe the biomechanical alignment characteristics of proper lower body landing mechanics in both the sagittal and frontal planes, and explain the injury consequences of dynamic knee valgus.  
**Model Answer:** In the sagittal plane, proper landing mechanics require the athlete to maintain their center of gravity directly over their base of support. The shoulders should be positioned directly over the knees, and the knees positioned over the toes. This alignment is accomplished through synchronized, active flexion of the ankles, knees, and hips (triple flexion), which allows eccentric muscular work to dissipate ground reaction forces. In the frontal plane, the knees must track directly over the toes without medial or lateral deviation. Inward movement or medial collapse of the knees—termed dynamic knee valgus—places excessive shear and rotational stress on the tibiofemoral joint, dramatically elevating the risk of patellofemoral pain syndrome and catastrophic tears or ruptures of the anterior cruciate ligament (ACL).  
**Evaluation Criteria:**  
- Detail sagittal alignment: shoulders over knees, knees over toes, center of gravity over base of support via triple flexion (ankle, knee, hip).  
- Detail frontal alignment: knees tracking directly over toes without medial collapse.  
- Define dynamic valgus and identify its clinical consequences: patellofemoral pain and ACL tears/ruptures.

21. Contrast the historical 1RM squat strength recommendation for plyometrics with modern evidence-based guidelines, and outline the balance testing protocol described in Table 18.7.  
**Model Answer:** Historically, strength and conditioning guidelines held that an athlete must possess a 1RM back squat of at least 1.5 times their body weight before being permitted to perform lower body plyometrics. Modern evidence-based practice recognizes that movement quality, technical proficiency, and proper landing mechanics are far more critical than an arbitrary strength threshold. Progressively teaching landing alignment and deceleration has repeatedly been shown to decrease injury rates in practices and games, making progressive plyometrics safe and beneficial even for novice and youth athletes. To ensure adequate postural stability, Table 18.7 outlines a standardized balance testing protocol consisting of three 30-second tests: standing, quarter squat, and half squat, performed on the same surface as training. Beginners must hold a single-leg standing position for 30 seconds without falling, whereas advanced athletes entering high-intensity cycles must hold a single-leg half squat for 30 seconds.  
**Evaluation Criteria:**  
- State historical strength rule: 1RM squat >= 1.5x body weight.  
- Explain modern view: technique, landing mechanics, and progressive overload supersede arbitrary 1RM ratios, safely reducing injury risk.  
- Detail Table 18.7 balance protocol: 30-second hold duration on training surface; contrast beginner (single-leg standing) with advanced (single-leg half squat).


## Part 2: Equipment, Facilities, and Depth Jump Safety Guidelines

### Multiple-Choice Questions (MCQs)

1. Which landing surfaces are recommended for lower body plyometric drills due to their adequate shock-absorbing properties?  
A. Concrete slabs and ceramic tile  
B. Grass fields, suspended floors, and dense rubber mats  
C. Unyielding solid hardwood and asphalt tracks  
D. Ice rinks and loose pea gravel  

**Answer: B. Grass fields, suspended floors, and dense rubber mats**  
**Rationale:** To prevent injuries, the landing surface used for lower body plyometrics must possess adequate shock-absorbing properties; a grass field, suspended floor, or rubber mat is a good surface choice.

2. Why are excessively thick exercise mats (6 inches [15 cm] or thicker) contraindicated for athletic plyometric training?  
A. They cause acute bone microfractures upon foot strike  
B. They extend the amortization phase and prevent efficient use of the stretch reflex  
C. They completely eliminate blood flow to the lower extremity  
D. They increase concentric contraction velocity beyond physiological limits  

**Answer: B. They extend the amortization phase and prevent efficient use of the stretch reflex**  
**Rationale:** Excessively thick exercise mats (6 inches [15 cm] or thicker) may extend the amortization phase and thus not allow efficient use of the stretch reflex, causing stored elastic energy to dissipate as heat.

3. While mini-trampolines are useful in rehabilitation, why are they ineffective for power training in uninjured athletes?  
A. The elastic surface extends the amortization phase, blunting stretch reflex potentiation  
B. They generate excessive gravitational impact forces that shatter bones  
C. They prevent the athlete from recruiting fast-twitch muscle fibers  
D. They induce permanent vestibular dysfunction  

**Answer: A. The elastic surface extends the amortization phase, blunting stretch reflex potentiation**  
**Rationale:** Mini-trampolines, like thick exercise mats, are not effective for plyometric training of uninjured athletes because the amortization phase is extended while the athlete is in contact with the elastic surface.

### Applied Short-Answer Questions

20. Explain the biomechanical and physiological reasons why excessively thick mats (>= 6 inches) and mini-trampolines are contraindicated for power development in uninjured athletes, despite their utility in rehabilitation.  
**Model Answer:** In rehabilitation, mini-trampolines and thick compliant mats provide a low-impact environment suitable for progressive joint reloading and balance retraining. However, they are contraindicated for athletic power development because plyometric efficacy depends strictly on a rapid stretch-shortening cycle (SSC) with an exceptionally brief amortization phase. When an uninjured athlete lands on an excessively thick mat (>= 6 inches [15 cm]) or a mini-trampoline, the compliant surface deforms and prolongs ground contact time, dramatically extending the amortization phase. Consequently, the strain elastic energy stored within the series elastic component (SEC/tendons) during the eccentric phase dissipates into the surrounding tissues as heat rather than contributing to concentric recoil. Furthermore, the electromechanical delay exceeds the physiological latency of the stretch reflex, preventing alpha motor neuron potentiation.  
**Evaluation Criteria:**  
- Distinguish rehabilitation utility (low impact, balance) from power training goals.  
- Explain that compliant surfaces extend contact time and prolong the amortization phase.  
- State the physiological consequences: SEC elastic energy dissipates as heat, and stretch reflex potentiation is lost.

21. Detail the equipment and facility guidelines for plyometric jump boxes and depth jumping, including box dimensions, construction standards, recommended drop height ranges, and restrictions for heavy athletes.  
**Model Answer:** Jump boxes used for box and depth jumps must be constructed from sturdy 3/4-inch (1.9 cm) plywood or heavy-gauge metal to prevent collapse under impact. The landing top must measure at least 18 by 24 inches (46 by 61 cm) and feature a nonslip surface created by rubberized flooring, nonslip treads, or sand mixed into paint. Box heights range from 6 to 42 inches (15 to 107 cm). For depth jumps, the recommended height range is 16 to 42 inches (41 to 107 cm), with 30 to 32 inches (76 to 81 cm) serving as the standard norm for conditioned athletes. Heights of 48 inches (1.2 m) are contraindicated because the excessive impact force prolongs the amortization phase and defeats the purpose of the drill. Finally, athletes weighing over 220 pounds (100 kg) must not perform depth jumps from heights greater than 18 inches (46 cm) due to elevated joint compressive loads.  
**Evaluation Criteria:**  
- Detail box construction (3/4-inch plywood or heavy metal), surface area (>= 18x24 in), and nonslip surface methods.  
- State depth jump height range (16–42 in) and standard norm (30–32 in).  
- Explain why 48-inch drops are contraindicated (excessive impact, prolonged amortization).  
- State the height restriction for athletes > 220 lbs (<= 18 inches).
